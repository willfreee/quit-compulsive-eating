The Easy Way to Stop Compulsive Eating

Chapter 15: The Diet Trap: How Restriction Feeds the Monster

It's Sunday night.

Tomorrow is Monday. And you've made the decision again. Tomorrow, everything changes—no more sugar, no more carbs, no more eating after seven. Tomorrow, you're going to be good, disciplined, finally in control.

And so tonight, you eat everything you can find—because it's your last chance before the deprivation begins.

The irony is almost too perfect. The diet hasn't even started yet, and it's already causing a binge.

But you don't see it that way. You see the binge as evidence of why you need the diet. You see your lack of control as proof that more control is required. You see the "last supper" as a final indulgence before the discipline begins—not as a warning sign that the discipline itself is the problem.

What if the diet isn't the solution to your bingeing? What if the diet is what's causing it?

The Drowning Man and the Anchor

Let's start with the appeal of the diet.

It's seductive. I understand why you keep coming back to it. The diet offers structure, certainty, rules. It tells you exactly what to eat and what to avoid. It gives you a sense of control in a relationship with food that has felt chaotic and unmanageable.

Starting a new diet feels like being handed a map after years of wandering lost—the clean slate, the clear guidelines, the relief of knowing what's allowed and what's forbidden. For the first few days—sometimes the first few weeks—it works. You follow the rules, feel virtuous, lose a few pounds. People notice. You think: This time is different. This time I've finally found the answer.

But here's what you need to understand: the answer was never the diet. The diet was always the problem.

Imagine a person who is drowning. They're thrashing in the water, desperate for something to hold onto. Someone throws them an anchor—heavy, solid, feeling like security. They grab it with both hands and feel a surge of relief: I'm holding something. I'm not drowning anymore.

But the anchor doesn't keep them afloat. The anchor pulls them deeper.

The diet is the anchor. It feels like security. It feels like control. But it's not keeping you afloat—it's dragging you under. And the longer you hold onto it, the deeper you sink.

The Pink Elephant

But let's look at what the diet actually does.

You might argue that restriction works. When I forbid a food, I eat less of it. The rules keep me in check.

And for a while, that's true. The first days of a diet are often marked by strict adherence. You feel virtuous, in control, like you've finally cracked the code.

But then something happens. Slowly at first, then with increasing intensity, the forbidden foods start calling to you. You find yourself thinking about them more and more—dreaming about them, obsessing over them. The food you never cared about before the diet has suddenly become the most important thing in your world.

Why?

Because restriction creates artificial scarcity.

Imagine someone telling you not to think about a pink elephant. The moment the rule is imposed, the pink elephant is everywhere. You can't stop thinking about it. The prohibition itself has made the pink elephant the center of your mental universe.

Food restrictions work the same way. The moment you declare a food forbidden, it becomes psychologically precious. The deprivation doesn't eliminate desire—it amplifies it. The forbidden fruit becomes the only fruit worth having.

This is not a failure of willpower. This is how the human mind works—when something is scarce, we want it more; when something is forbidden, we fixate on it. The diet doesn't make you want the food less—it makes you want it more.

The Beach Ball Underwater

And then comes the inevitable rebellion.

The binges happen because I'm weak. If I had more willpower, I could stick to the diet.

You've told yourself this a thousand times. Every time a diet collapses into a binge, you blame yourself—you weren't disciplined enough, you weren't strong enough, you failed.

But here's the truth: the binge was never a failure of willpower. The binge was physics.

Imagine holding a beach ball underwater. You can do it—for a while. Your arms are strong, your determination genuine. The ball stays submerged. But the longer you hold it down, the more force builds. The ball strains against your hands, your muscles tire. And eventually, inevitably, the ball erupts from the water with explosive force.

Was the eruption a failure of your strength? Or was it the inevitable result of trying to hold something underwater that was always going to surface?

The binge is the beach ball. The restriction is the hand holding it underwater. And the eruption isn't a choice—it's a consequence. The diet creates the very thing it promises to prevent.

The "last supper" before starting a new diet is not a lapse in discipline. It's the body and mind rebelling against the coming deprivation. It's the beach ball sensing the pressure before it's even applied.

Every period of restriction guarantees a period of rebellion. The only question is when.

The Locked Box

Now let's talk about the food moralizing.

Some foods are genuinely bad. Sugar is bad. Carbs are bad. I need to label them as forbidden to protect myself.

You've been taught to categorize foods into "good" and "bad," "clean" and "dirty," "allowed" and "forbidden." You've been taught to attach moral weight to eating—virtue when you eat the "good" foods, guilt when you eat the "bad" ones.

But here's the question: what does the label actually do?

Imagine two cookies. They're identical—same ingredients, same taste, same size. One is on a plate marked "EAT ME." The other is in a locked box marked "FORBIDDEN."

Which one do you want more?

The cookies are identical. But the label changes everything. The forbidden cookie becomes irresistible precisely because it's forbidden. The locked box creates the desire.

This is what food moralizing does. It doesn't protect you from "bad" foods—it makes those foods psychologically irresistible. The label creates the desire. The prohibition fuels the obsession.

The Big Monster feeds on the very rules designed to starve it. Every time you declare a food forbidden, you hand the Big Monster another weapon. Every time you attach moral weight to eating, you deepen the trap.

The cookies were always just cookies. The labels were always just labels. And the power you gave them was never real—it was manufactured by the rules you imposed.

The Snake Eating Its Tail

Now let's look at the full cycle.

Restriction → Deprivation → Desire → Rebellion → Binge → Guilt → Renewed Determination → Stricter Restriction → Stronger Deprivation → More Violent Rebellion.

Over and over. Each rotation tightening the trap.

Diets fail because I fail. The diet was fine—I was the problem. I just need to try harder next time.

This is the most insidious lie the diet mentality tells. It convinces you that the diet was never the problem—you were. The diet was perfect. You were weak. The solution is more discipline, more restriction, more control.

But the truth is the opposite. The diet created the binge. The binge created the guilt. The guilt created the next diet. And each rotation makes the binges worse and the restrictions more desperate.

Imagine a snake eating its own tail. The more it eats, the hungrier it gets; the more it consumes, the more it needs. The cycle doesn't lead anywhere—it just tightens.

The diet-binge cycle is that snake. It's not a series of personal failures. It's a self-perpetuating system. The Parasite doesn't need to do anything—the cycle runs itself.

The Casino

But maybe you're thinking: Diets work for other people. I've seen people lose weight and keep it off. Why can't I do what they did?

Let's look at that observation more closely.

You've seen people lose weight on diets. You've seen the before-and-after photos. You've heard the success stories. And you've concluded that diets work—just not for you.

But here's what the diet industry doesn't want you to notice: the success stories are part of the business model.

Imagine a casino. Some people win. The casino makes sure of it—the occasional jackpot keeps the hopeful coming back. The winner's story spreads. "I won big! You can too!" And the losing masses keep feeding the machines, convinced that their jackpot is coming.

But the house always wins in the long run.

The diet industry works the same way. The occasional success story—usually temporary, usually followed by weight regain—keeps the hopeful coming back. The person who lost weight on keto or intermittent fasting or whatever the current trend happens to be becomes a walking advertisement. And the failing masses, convinced that they just haven't found the right diet yet, keep paying for the next one.

If diets worked permanently, the diet industry would collapse. The entire business model depends on failure. The diets aren't designed to free you. They're designed to keep you coming back.

The Open Door

So what's the alternative?

If I give myself total permission to eat anything, I'll eat everything. I'll lose all control. Total permission is a recipe for disaster.

This is the fear that keeps the diet cycle running. The terror of removing all rules. The belief that without restrictions, you would descend into chaos.

But let me offer you a different image.

Imagine a prisoner who has been locked in a cell for years. The cell is small. The walls are familiar. The routine is predictable. Then one day, the door is opened. The prisoner is told they're free.

They hesitate.

"If I leave," they think, "I'll just wander. I won't know what to do. I'll be lost. The cell is safe. The cell gives me structure. What if I can't handle the freedom?"

Is the cell protecting them? Or is the fear of freedom just another bar of the cage?

Total permission is not chaos. It's the only path to genuine freedom. When food is no longer forbidden, it loses its power. When nothing is restricted, nothing needs to be rebelled against. When the rules dissolve, the deprivation that fueled the binges dissolves with them.

The beach ball doesn't need to be held underwater anymore. It can float freely. And when it floats freely, it doesn't erupt.

The Big Monster starves without the diet to feed it. The Parasite dies without the cycle to sustain it. And you—the real you, the one who has been trapped in the cell for so long—finally step through the open door.

Freedom is not the absence of rules. It's the absence of the need for rules.

So the diet is the trap. The restriction is the engine. The rules are the fuel. And the only way out is total permission. But what does total permission actually feel like? How do you live without rules, without restrictions, without the constant mental chatter about what's allowed and what's forbidden?

We're going to see what happens when the food crutch dissolves and you're left with something far more powerful: the ability to experience all of life's emotional waves without panic, without defense mechanisms, without substitute crutches. We're going to discover what genuine freedom actually feels like.

The cage door is open. Let's see what's outside.
